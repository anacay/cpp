*The Operator is a fictional company. Any resemblance to real companies reflects problems the industry shares.*

This is the practice's second problem-space case, and the first with a full answer beside it. The case sets out the problem in full and prescribes nothing. A companion page then works the problem through the practice's body of knowledge, and names where that body of knowledge runs out.

## The situation

The Operator runs one of the largest computing fleets in the world. It plans about $200 billion of capital spending in 2026 and still calls itself supply constrained. Every year it harvests millions of components from the machines it retires, over 3 million of them drives in 2024. How many of those are solid-state drives isn't public. Older flash also sits idle inside machines that are still running.

New enterprise flash now costs three to four times what it did a year earlier, and next year's supply is largely pledged. So the old drives look valuable. Each one can go four ways: into a workload that tolerates it, into the pool of repair spares, into an upgrade or a new build, or out the door through resale, recycling or the shredder.

No public source shows the Operator running a program for this. The public record shows every ingredient such a program would need, and every seam it would fall through.

## Why it is hard

- **Nobody has said what kind of value it is.** If the Operator's purchase commitments can flex, a reused drive avoids a purchase. If they are firm, it adds capacity to a sold-out fleet. Those are different numbers, with different beneficiaries.
- **Nobody knows how big it is.** The drive harvest could be under 1% or near 30% of a year's flash deployment (illustrative). That decides whether this is spares housekeeping or supply strategy.
- **Supply is a byproduct.** Drives come back when a platform retires, for reasons that have nothing to do with drives. Keeping a platform alive raises its need for spares while cutting the harvest that would feed them.
- **A drive has five dimensions, and every sink prices a different one.** Capacity, performance, remaining endurance, fit and condition history. No common unit or price exists inside the fleet.
- **Nobody has published how reused drives behave in a second life.** The evidence suggests parity early, then rising and correlated failure. One bad incident could cost more than the program saves.
- **The organization splits the problem in two.** The people who allocate capacity and the people who recover value sit in different organizations. The technicians who pull drives are measured on ticket speed.

## What's in the case

- The system: where old flash sits, how it moves, and the gates between a retiring host and a second life.
- Demand: the four places a drive can go, and what each one prices.
- The money: a scale model from the market down to one drive, and the fork between avoided purchases and added capacity.
- Two futures for the flash market, with the signposts between them.
- The planning organization as part of the problem.
- Eight decisions with owners and links, eighteen causal chains and seven stress scenarios for 2027.
- Measures of success, a confidence ledger, and sixteen open questions with falsifiers.
- What other industries and the research literature learned first.

Every claim carries an evidence tier, so you can see which parts are reported, inferred or speculated.

## How the practice solves it

The companion page works the case through the seed paper, decision by decision. It starts by sizing the problem and deciding what kind of value it is, before designing anything. It then audits the paper's thirteen mechanisms against the case and sets out a gated sequence, with owners, cadences, measures and an experiment for every stated assumption.

Where the practice runs out, the page says so. The largest gap is supply that comes back out of the fleet in parts, of uncertain condition, as a byproduct of other decisions. That gap now has its own working note, [Second lives](note-second-lives.md).
